Current — commentary Punjab · posted 22.08.2026

Punjab & the dissenting voice A regional tradition of questioning power, read against India’s constitutional design.

Dissent, in India’s constitutional design, is not a liberty extended to citizens at the State’s discretion; it is a condition the Constitution itself presupposes. Article 19(1)(a) guarantees freedom of speech and expression not as an ornamental right but as the mechanism by which the exercise of power is examined and, where necessary, questioned.

Punjab’s own history illustrates the point. A region shaped by Partition, by agrarian struggle, and by repeated confrontation with the machinery of the State has long treated the right to disagree as inseparable from civic life. That inheritance is not incidental to constitutional government; it is one of the conditions that makes constitutional government answerable.

The Supreme Court has said as much, repeatedly. In Kedar Nath Singh v. State of Bihar, the Court confined the offence of sedition to acts inciting violence or public disorder, holding that strong criticism of government, however sharply expressed, remains within the protection of Article 19(1)(a). In S. Rangarajan v. P. Jagjivan Ram, it described open discussion as the tradition on which a democracy is nurtured. In Shreya Singhal v. Union of India, it struck down a provision criminalising speech for causing mere “annoyance” or “inconvenience,” reaffirming that the Constitution does not permit disagreement to be treated as disorder.

None of this is an argument for disorder, and none of it is partisan. It is a statement of constitutional structure: a democracy that suppresses the right to question does not thereby become more orderly; it becomes less accountable. Punjab’s dissenting voice, read against this line of authority, is not an exception to India’s constitutional story. It is part of how that story is meant to work.

Current — commentary Punjab · posted 21.08.2026

New facts, 2015: evidence, an attack, and a question of sanction A sequence of dated, documented events that, taken together, calls for independent examination.

On 8 May 2025, new evidence concerning the 2015 sacrilege case was placed before a court in Chandigarh. Three weeks later, on 1 June 2025, the person who had placed it, journalist and law graduate Pushp Sharma, was attacked. The incident was investigated by Punjab Police and entered in the police diary of 18 June 2025.

Before the High Court of Punjab & Haryana, the State’s position was that Pushp Sharma had not approached the concerned authorities seeking security. Set against that diary entry, the position invites an obvious question: on what record was it made?

The sequence does not end there. Sharma had sought registration of an FIR against senior State officials, raising the requirement of prior sanction under Section 197, Code of Criminal Procedure. On 8 July 2026, the Home Department of Punjab passed a Speaking Order stating that Government could not, at its own level, generally dispense with that statutory requirement, and that the question of sanction could be considered only upon a proposal from the concerned investigating or prosecuting agency.

Each fact, taken alone, may have an innocent explanation. Taken together — evidence produced, its producer attacked within weeks, a diary entry the State’s own submission appears not to account for, and a sanction requirement that leaves the question of an FIR dependent on the same machinery the FIR would investigate — the sequence is not one a court, or the public, should have to accept on trust.

What is sought is not a presumption of guilt against any official. It is an independent examination: of the evidence placed on 8 May 2025, of the investigation into 1 June 2025, and of the basis for the State’s submission to the High Court. Where allegations touch senior public office, institutional independence is not a courtesy. It is the only way the process survives its own scrutiny.

Current — commentary Punjab · posted 21.08.2026

The cost of a flawed law Judicial review corrects a statute. On its own, it does not make the State answer for having got it wrong.

Parliament makes laws, but when a demonstrably flawed law unjustifiably harms life and liberty, the State must bear constitutional accountability.

The presumption of constitutionality that attaches to every enactment is a rule of judicial restraint. It is not a certificate of legislative infallibility. Statutes are drafted, debated and passed by fallible institutions, and constitutional history records enough provisions later declared arbitrary, vague or disproportionate to confirm that the presumption can, and does, fail.

The difficulty lies in the interval between enactment and correction. A citizen prosecuted, detained or otherwise deprived of liberty under a law subsequently struck down or read down does not recover the years spent answering that prosecution, the livelihood lost to it, or the reputational injury already sustained. Judicial review, indispensable as it is, operates prospectively and declaratively. It tells the State that a law was wrong. It does not require the State to answer for having enacted or enforced it. The remedy corrects the statute book. It does not compensate the citizen, and it imposes no cost on the lawmaker.

That asymmetry is the deterrence problem. A public authority that bears no cost for an arbitrary measure has no institutional reason to legislate more carefully the next time. Article 21’s guarantee of life and personal liberty according to procedure established by law was never meant to tolerate procedure that is itself arbitrary; Maneka Gandhi settled that much in principle. Compensatory jurisprudence for custodial excess has existed since Rudul Sah and Nilabati Behera — a constitutional wrong without a remedy, the Supreme Court held, is no remedy at all. That same logic has yet to be extended, consistently, to harm flowing from a flawed law itself, as distinct from its misapplication by an errant official.

Judicial review corrects the law. Only accountability — cost, compensation, consequence — corrects the incentive to legislate carelessly. Without that cost, arbitrariness is not deterred. It is, at best, occasionally undone, at the citizen’s expense.

Current matter — open Representation served 14.08.2026 · reply due within four weeks

Panjab University: who may rewrite the Senate? Three notifications in ten days, and a question of law put to the Union Government before any Court is approached.

In October and November 2025 the Central Government issued, rescinded, reissued and again rescinded a set of directions that would have remade the governing bodies of Panjab University — not by amending the Punjab University Act, 1947 in Parliament, but by notification under Section 72 of the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966. The notifications are gone. The question of power is not.

What the notifications did

  1. 28.10.2025 S.O. 4933(E) — directions issued under Section 72, to take effect as from the date of issue of this notification. Ordinary Fellows capped at twenty-four, with the election of any Ordinary Fellow made subject to the approval of the Chancellor; section 14 omitted; sections 19 and 20 substituted, vesting executive government in a Syndicate of the Vice-Chancellor as Chairperson, the Secretary (Higher Education) Government of India, the Directors of Public Instruction for Punjab and Chandigarh, one Senate member nominated by the Chancellor, and ten members nominated by the Vice-Chancellor; section 37 omitted; and the Schedule of ex officio Fellows replaced with a list of seven. Published at Gazette No. 4782 of 30.10.2025, CG-DL-E-01112025-267323 · F. No. 2-6/2021-U.II.
  2. 04.11.2025 S.O. 5022(E) — rescinds S.O. 4933(E).
  3. 04.11.2025 S.O. 5023(E) — reimposes the same modifications on the same day, but to take effect from the date, as appointed by the Central Government. The substance is identical to S.O. 4933(E). What changes is commencement: immediate effect is replaced by a date left to be appointed. The restructuring is not withdrawn so much as held in suspense.
  4. 07.11.2025 S.O. 5063(E) — rescinds S.O. 5023(E), restoring the position as it stood before 28.10.2025.

Why it still matters

Section 72(1) permits the Central Government to issue directions for the continued functioning of the University. Section 72(2) permits any such direction to provide that the governing law shall have effect subject to such exceptions and modifications as may be specified in the direction. Neither sub-section says how far a “modification” may go. In Rajnarain Singh v. Chairman, Patna Administration Committee, AIR 1954 SC 569, construing comparable language, the Supreme Court held that a power of modification cannot be used to alter the essential features or policy of the law being modified. Whether that principle governs Section 72 has never been decided.

Because the notifications were withdrawn, no Court has ruled on the limits of the power. The power therefore remains available, on the same terms, to be exercised again — by this Government or any other.

The two questions put to the Union

  • Whether the power to make ‘exceptions and modifications’ under Section 72(2) of the Punjab Reorganisation Act, 1966, extends to altering the essential features or legislative policy of the statutory governance structure of Panjab University, including the elected/representative character of its Senate and Syndicate?
  • Whether Section 72 permits the Central Government to alter the statutory governance structure in a manner that enables the Government of the day, irrespective of the political party in office, to materially influence or control the composition of the University’s governing institutions through nomination or lateral induction?

The representation asks that the answer be given by a duly authorised and sworn affidavit or verified statement, setting out the express and implied legal limits of the power. It does not assert that the 2025 notifications were beyond power; it asks the Union to state where the boundary lies, before any Court is moved.

Future course of action

If no substantive reply is received within one month of service — by 14.09.2026 — or if the reply received does not address the two questions set out above, the matter is proposed to be placed before the High Court of Punjab & Haryana under Article 226 of the Constitution, seeking the Court’s determination of the scope of the modification power under Section 72.

This step is contingent, not pre-decided. The representation remains, in the first instance, an invitation to the Union Government to state its own position before any Court is asked to.

An invitation

Students, journalists, academics, activists, and members of the public with an interest in university governance and constitutional accountability are invited to follow this matter as it develops, to read the underlying notifications and representation for themselves, and to raise the same or related questions through their own independent channels — with their own institutions, elected representatives, or the same authorities addressed here.

This is not an invitation to join as co-petitioners or to instruct counsel through this page. It is an invitation to informed attention. A university’s Senate and Syndicate are not an administrative footnote — they are where the question of who governs a public university gets settled, notification by notification. That question belongs to more people than one petitioner.

Legal position on record

10

Reviving a judicially closed FIR

Set out below is a statement of legal position, prepared for reference in future proceedings. It is not a pleading in any pending matter and is not directed at, or a comment on the facts of, any specific case.

1. Further investigation is not reinvestigation

Once a closure report has been accepted by the competent Magistrate, any subsequent investigation must remain within the statutory concept of “further investigation” under Section 193(9), BNSS, 2023 (corresponding to Section 173(8), CrPC, 1973). It cannot be converted into a fresh, de novo, or reinvestigative exercise merely to revisit the same material. A fresh or de novo investigation — one conducted afresh from the beginning, substantially replacing rather than supplementing the earlier investigation — is recognised as an exceptional power of the superior constitutional courts, not a power the investigating agency or the State may exercise of its own volition: Vinay Tyagi v. Irshad Ali, (2013) 5 SCC 762.

2. Judicial closure is not an executively reversible decision

Where a closure report has been judicially accepted, the State cannot treat the FIR as an open-ended executive proceeding. Any proposed further investigation must remain within the statutory framework and under prior judicial oversight. The Supreme Court has since held, expressly, that leave of the Magistrate or Court is a necessary prerequisite to further investigation after a final report has been submitted: Pramod Kumar & Ors. v. State of Uttar Pradesh, 2026 INSC 120 (04.02.2026); reaffirmed in Paliniswamy Veeraraja & Ors. v. State of Karnataka & Anr., Crl. Appeal arising out of SLP (Crl.) No. 16149/2024 (2026).

3. Further investigation requires further evidence, not merely a change of view

The State must be able to identify the further oral or documentary evidence, or other legitimate investigative basis, that warrants continuation. A mere change in administrative, political, or investigative approach does not by itself convert material already examined into fresh justification for reopening the matter. Further investigation is intended to supplement the original investigation, not to re-evaluate it as a fresh one.

4. Colourable exercise of power and mala fides

Where revival of an old FIR is accompanied by circumstances suggesting the criminal process is being used for an extraneous purpose — retaliation, harassment, political vendetta, or sustained pressure — the exercise is open to scrutiny under Articles 14 and 21 and the settled principles governing abuse of process. The relevant question is not merely whether the State possesses investigative power, but whether that power is being exercised for the purpose for which the law confers it.

5. Delay, prejudice and abuse of process

The longer the interval between the original closure and an attempted revival, the greater the need for the State to explain the intervening circumstances, the basis for the proposed further investigation, and why the relevant material could not reasonably have been pursued earlier. Where revival causes serious prejudice or operates as continuing harassment, the High Court’s jurisdiction to prevent abuse of process may be invoked.

The controlling principle

The State cannot convert “further investigation” into a mechanism for keeping an old FIR permanently alive. The power to investigate is not an unlimited power to repeatedly reopen a matter merely because a later administration or investigating officer takes a different view of material already examined.

The test, applied cumulatively

  1. What further evidence or material has emerged?
  2. Why does that material warrant further investigation now?
  3. Is the proposed exercise genuinely further investigation, or reinvestigation/de novo investigation in substance?
  4. Has the required judicial oversight — particularly prior leave of the Magistrate or Court — been obtained?
  5. Is the power being exercised for a legitimate investigative purpose, or as a continuing instrument of State pressure?

A finding that the exercise is reinvestigation or de novo investigation in substance, or that the required prior judicial leave was not obtained, is a ground for challenge. Independently, if the surrounding circumstances establish that the power is being exercised for an extraneous, mala fide, or colourable purpose, the exercise may be vulnerable as an abuse of process.

An old FIR does not become a perpetual instrument of executive pressure merely because the State retains the statutory power of further investigation. Judicial closure does not necessarily immunise a case from lawful further investigation — but it does require the State to remain within the statutory limits of that power and under judicial supervision. This statement is prepared as a general reference and is not, and should not be read as, a claim that any particular authority has acted or intends to act in the manner described.

FIR Recurrence: Where Does Further Investigation End?

Brief. A closed FIR can be subjected to further investigation with prior judicial permission, but the law does not clearly prescribe how many times it may be reopened.
