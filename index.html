Grounds to Challenge the State's
Attempt to Revive a Judicially Closed FIR

The further-investigation / de novo distinction, and a contemporaneous illustration of reopening as proxy score-settling

Prepared 15.08.2026 · India · Criminal Procedure

Once a closure report has been judicially accepted, the State's residual power to investigate further is not an unlimited licence to reopen the matter at will. Six grounds, a cumulative test, and a live illustration follow.

Grounds
01

Further Investigation Is Not Reinvestigation

Once an 'A' Summary/closure report has been accepted by the competent Magistrate, any subsequent investigation must remain within the statutory concept of "further investigation" under Section 193(9) BNSS (corresponding to Section 173(8) CrPC); it cannot be converted into a fresh, de novo, or reinvestigative exercise merely to revisit the same material.

De novo investigation, in one line: an entirely fresh investigation conducted from the beginning, substantially replacing or superseding the earlier investigation rather than continuing and supplementing it.

Vinay Tyagi v. Irshad Ali, (2013) 5 SCC 762, distinguishes further investigation from reinvestigation and recognises that fresh/de novo investigation is an exceptional power of the superior constitutional courts — it is not a power the investigating agency or the State may exercise on its own volition.

02

Judicial Closure Is Not an Executively Reversible Decision

Where a closure report has been judicially accepted, the State cannot treat the FIR as an open-ended executive proceeding. Any proposed further investigation must remain within the statutory framework and be subject to prior judicial oversight. The Supreme Court, in Pramod Kumar & Ors. v. State of Uttar Pradesh, 2026 INSC 120 (decided 04.02.2026), has now expressly held — drawing on Vinay Tyagi — that prior leave of the Magistrate/Court is a mandatory prerequisite for further investigation after a final report has been submitted and accepted, and that this power vests solely in the Magistrate or a superior court, not in the investigating agency or executive authorities. This position has since been reiterated in Paliniswamy Veeraraja & Ors. v. State of Karnataka (2026), where a chargesheet arising from further investigation conducted without such leave was quashed as an abuse of process.

03

Further Investigation Requires Further Evidence, Not Merely a Change of View

The State should be able to identify the further oral or documentary evidence, or other legitimate investigative basis, that warrants continuation of the investigation. A mere change in administrative, political, or investigative approach cannot, by itself, convert material already examined into a fresh justification for reopening the matter. Further investigation is intended to supplement the original investigation, not to replace or re-evaluate it as a fresh investigation.

04

Colourable Exercise of Power and Mala Fides

Where revival of an old FIR is accompanied by circumstances suggesting that the criminal process is being used for an extraneous purpose — retaliation, harassment, political vendetta, or sustained pressure — the exercise is open to scrutiny under Articles 14 and 21 and the settled principles governing abuse of process, including the categories identified in State of Haryana v. Bhajan Lal, 1992 Supp (1) SCC 335. The relevant question is not merely whether the State possesses investigative power, but whether that power is being exercised for the purpose for which the law confers it.

05

Delay, Prejudice and Abuse of Process

The longer the interval between the original closure and the attempted revival, the greater the need for the State to explain the intervening circumstances, the basis for the proposed further investigation, and why the relevant material could not reasonably have been pursued earlier. Where revival causes serious prejudice or operates as continuing harassment, the High Court's jurisdiction to prevent abuse of process may be invoked.

06

A Contemporaneous Illustration: Reopening as Proxy Score-Settling

The concern at Ground 4 is not merely theoretical. It is presently live before a constitutional court.

Arnab Goswami v. State of Maharashtra & Ors.Pending · Bombay HC

The petition seeks quashing of a 2018 FIR alleging abetment of suicide, closed by way of an 'A' Summary report accepted by the Chief Judicial Magistrate, Alibaug, in 2019, and reopened by the State in 2020. Hearing the matter on 14.08.2026, Justice Milind Jadhav narrowed the issue to precisely the question at Ground 1–2: "whether the state has power to re-open and in what circumstances." The Bench cautioned that legal proceedings "should not become a 'proxy kind of war'," and observed that "much water has flown since 2020" — a judicial recognition of the delay/prejudice concern at Ground 5. The petitioner's case, that the reopening followed a change in the State's political dispensation and coincided with hostile assembly proceedings against him, is the fact pattern Ground 4 is written to test.

Synthesis

The Controlling Principle

The State cannot convert "further investigation" into a mechanism for keeping an old FIR permanently alive. The power to investigate is not an unlimited power to repeatedly reopen a matter merely because a later administration or investigating officer takes a different view of material already examined.

The Test — To Be Applied Cumulatively

  1. What further evidence or material has emerged?
  2. Why does that material warrant further investigation now?
  3. Is the proposed exercise genuinely "further investigation," or is it reinvestigation/de novo investigation in substance?
  4. Has the required judicial oversight — particularly prior leave of the Magistrate/Court — been obtained?
  5. Is the power being exercised for a legitimate investigative purpose, or as a continuing instrument of State pressure?

Operative effect

A finding that the exercise is reinvestigation/de novo investigation in substance, or that the required prior judicial leave was not obtained, provides a substantial procedural ground to challenge and quash the action. Independently, where the surrounding circumstances establish that the investigative power is being exercised for an extraneous, mala fide, or colourable purpose, the exercise is vulnerable as an abuse of process.

The Principle for Future Cases

An old FIR cannot become a perpetual instrument of executive pressure merely because the State retains the statutory power of further investigation. Judicial closure does not necessarily immunise a case from lawful further investigation — but it does require the State to remain within the statutory limits of that power and under judicial supervision.
